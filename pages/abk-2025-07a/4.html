Dr Emily Remnant presented about the latest research into new more

targeted miticides, including double stranded RNA (see news item in this

issue), and very targeted chemicals designed to bind very specifically to

mite hormone receptors. These are promising developments as no one

wants to continue putting toxic chemicals in hives for the rest of eternity.

Apresenter at some point, trying to assuage a perceived fear of technology

amongst beekeepers while promoting a phone app, urged the audience

“look, you're already using technology! How to you pay for fuel?” and

without waiting for an answer provided one himself, “with your phone!”

I thought was emblematic of the deep disconnect between people

who love technology and those of us who area

little more traditional.

I think

I'm probably on the upper half of beekeeper tech sawviness andI still think

the fact that

I can just tap my card against a machine to pay for my fuel is

a technological marvel, and the idea of letting my phone pay for things willy

nilly gives me a deep sense of unease, so it’s probably not the best note

reach out to non-tech-savvy-beekeepers on.

I Know beekeepers that don’t

even have cell phones, so don’t you tech savvy people go thinking we're all

on the same page (of our paper notebooks). Anyway,

I digress.

Ballina local Frewoini Baume presented about Tropilaelaps, a mite that

is potentially worse than varroa (!) and currently on the spread throughout

the world. She has lately been traveling the world studying it with leading

researchers and gaining very valuable and enviable experience. On the

second day Dr Cooper Schouten also presented on Tropilaelaos, and

there is certainly enough to say about it that both presentations were very

interesting and informative.

Dr Sasha Mikheyev was able to present the results of our first Colony

Loss Survey (COLOSS), the most concisely interesting take away

I found

to be that beekeepers with varroa spent $27 and 32 minutes per hive last

year managing it the mite.
