President’s Report, cont’d

One Voice. VAA has worked hard over the past

twelve months to ensure Victoria’s beekeepers are

heard clearly and credibly. Government is listening.

Agriculture is listening. Policy makers are listening.

That has only been possible because our advocacy is

increasingly united, evidence-based and solutions-

focused.

One Guardian. Varroa remains the defining chal-

lenge of our generation. VAA will continue fighting to

ensure Victoria’s beekeepers have access to treatment

pathways that are legal, practical, safe and effective.

We must move quickly toward a future where every

Victorian beekeeper, regardless of scale, has confi-

dence in the treatment options available to them.

This work is well underway and will remain one of

VAA’s highest priorities.

A stake in the ground

The next twelve months must be a year of renewal.

My hope is that we continue to see younger leaders step

forward, ready to serve, support and shape the beekeep-

ing industry via VAA. My expectation is that Victo-

ria’s beekeepers continue to unite around practical, evi-

dence-based varroa management. My commitment is

that VAA will continue pushing for the systems, educa-

tion and regulatory settings required for our industry to

thrive.

I remain incredibly proud to serve as VAA presi-

dent. Thank you to every board member, volunteer,

sponsor, branch representative and member who has

contributed to this year’s progress. Together, we will

continue to build something stronger.

Kind regards,

Lindsay Callaway

President

Bags Of Dead Bees

By Kris Fricke

In the official training we provided we were told to

tell beekeepers after an alcohol wash to securely bag the

dead bees and dispose of them into the waste manage-

ment system. The legal basis for this is presumably

one or both of the following:

The Australian Honey Bee Industry Code of

Practice §7.1 – A beekeeper must not allow a used

hive, part of a used hive (including frames, combs,

honey or beeswax) or an appliance containing honey

to be exposed in a manner or under conditions likely

to attract robber bees; including during transporta-

tion.1

The Livestock Disease Control Act of 19942 also

has some related provisions:

§ 52 Exposure of bees to infected articles etc.

A person must not expose—

any hive infected with disease; or

any beeswax, fittings or other articles which are

infected with disease or have been taken from or

used in conjunction with any hive infected with dis-

ease— in any manner that will allow access by bees

to the hive or articles unless the hive or articles have

been disinfected.

A person must not expose or extract any honey

which is infected with disease in any manner which

will allow access to the honey by bees.

A person must not expose any pollen collected

from bees, which is infected with disease, in any

manner that will allow bees to access the pollen. A

beekeeper must take all reasonable precautions to

ensure that any honey, honeycomb, fittings or other

article on or in which honey is deposited and which

is in the person's possession or under the person's

control does not remain exposed in such a manner

or under such conditions that would provide bees

with access to the honey.

The Livestock and Disease Control Act of

20173 provides additional legal requirements for bee-

keepers but doesn’t touch on this question.

These are very good reasonable regulations every-

one should adhere to in order to prevent the spread of

disease. They are often paraphrased as “do not leave

any hive material exposed,” which is generally close

enough, but an astute reader may note that dead bees

soaked in alcohol are neither likely to attract robber

bees, nor are they infected with disease.

Consider that 1200-2400 bees are born in a hive eve-

ry day during the season, and therefore 1200-2400 bees

are also dying every day. Compared to this normal

background delivery of dead bees into the environment

by every single active hive, the addition of 300 dead

bees is not significant. Yes, it’s one ~30g clump,

which is similar to a dead mouse, which, incidentally, is

also a naturally occurring thing of little impact.

A thin film of alcohol will evaporate in less than an

hour, and alcohol is a naturally occurring substance that

definitely does not have any long-lasting toxic conse-

quences for the environment. Dumping out the remain-

ing alcohol could kill the grass it’s dumped on, but if

dumped into bare dirt should have no discernable nega-

tive consequences.

And so, if, after doing an alcohol wash you make a

small depression in the dirt with your heel, dispose of

the dead bees into it, and cover it again, there would be

no environmental or legal hazard.

Obviously, there’s nothing wrong with collecting all

your dead bees in a bag to throw in your trash at home,

but on the ecological balance that does introduce non-

biodegradable plastic into the waste system and the

dead bees are also now taking up space in the waste

system rather than quickly decomposing in the natural
